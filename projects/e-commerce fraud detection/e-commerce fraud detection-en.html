An early rapid-purchase subgroup accounts for 52.23% of fraud labels. The analysis compares recognition across behaviors and periods, then develops proposals for review prioritization and three-tier handling.

Executive summary

This project examines behavioral patterns in first-order fraud, compares model recognition, and considers how a risk team could allocate review effort and handle orders. The data contain 138,376 transactions and 1,415 fraud labels, with prevalence of 1.02%. All 739 purchases made within one second of signup carry fraud labels. This subgroup represents 52.23% of fraud labels and occurs in early January 2015.

Recognition differs sharply across this historical subgroup and the remaining transactions. The fit-pool random diagnostic has AP=0.635206, falling to 0.006314 when the one-second subgroup is excluded. Chronological evaluation tests the scope of these patterns. Forward validation selects weighted LightGBM, with frozen temporal-test AP=0.010121 and ROC-AUC=0.595995. Ordinary SMOTE improves neither CV nor test AP over the no-SMOTE comparator in this experiment.

Decision analysis separates ranking from action. The specified cost scenario selects approve-all on selection; none of the 131 test fraud labels is flagged, FP=0 and scenario cost is $6,876. The behavioral findings and decision trade-offs inform a proposal to prioritize human review by score and combine transaction evidence with low/medium/high handling tiers.

The first-order analysis objective

The task places a risk decision after a new customer's first order is submitted and before shipment. The risk team needs to find suspicious transactions while limiting friction for legitimate orders. The analysis asks which behavioral subgroups contain fraud, what the imbalance treatments recognize, and how scores could assist review and order handling.

Inputs include recorded signup and purchase times, value, account attributes, source, browser, and earlier device/IP transaction events. The binary class field supplies the fraud label. Each row is a recorded user transaction and user IDs are unique; the file cannot establish cross-account identity or genuine first-order eligibility.

The experiment uses retrospective chronological evaluation to compare approval and rejection under explicit cost scenarios. Behavioral findings inform proposals for human review and tiered handling.

The transaction and label sources

The transaction data contain 138,376 rows and 11 columns; the IP-range table contains 138,846 rows. The analysis focuses on first orders, with GMT timestamps and amounts in USD.

Purchases span January 1 through December 16, 2015; signup dates end on August 18. Fields cover user ID, both timestamps, value, device, source, browser, sex, age, numeric IP and target. There are no missing values, duplicate rows or duplicate user IDs. Values range from $9 to $154 and ages from 18 to 76. Target values are zero and one, timestamps parse successfully, and purchases never precede signup. The original file is not purchase-time sorted; 780 rows belong to tied purchase timestamps.

IP mapping first checks that lookup intervals do not overlap. searchsorted finds the greatest lower bound not exceeding each IP, followed by an upper-bound check. There are 20,291 unmapped rows, or 14.66%. Fractional values occur in 138,375 IP records; mapping retains the supplied numeric values, whose encoding provenance is unknown. Lookup results do not verify physical location. Country is descriptive only because lookup vintage is also unknown.

There are 1,415 fraud labels and 136,961 nonfraud labels, for overall prevalence of 1.02%. Approving every record would yield high accuracy, so accuracy does not select models. The transaction-time distributions

All 739 purchases within one second of signup carry fraud labels. They occur from January 1 through January 13, 2015, and account for 52.23% of all fraud labels. None occurs in the forward-validation windows, selection or test. Random splitting distributes this early pattern into both training and validation and can repeatedly recognize the same historical structure.

The disappearance of instant purchases, lengthening signup-to-purchase intervals and changes in prior-device activity are observable feature-distribution changes. Signups end in August while purchases continue into December, limiting interpretation of the later population. Monthly source, browser, age and amount distributions describe sample composition without identifying its generating mechanism.

Population composition, feature drift and label-prevalence shift describe different phenomena. Concept or regime change concerns the conditional feature-label relationship. This analysis does not independently identify that mechanism or establish a change in fraud strategy.

For review design, an extremely short purchase interval is an evidence-backed investigation cue. Its frequency, fraud prevalence and timing should be tracked together; the strong early association does not establish a permanent rejection rule.

The prediction-time features

The goal

Represent information observable at order submission and avoid treating future transaction volume as prior activity.

The feature contract

Numeric and calendar features are log1p signup-to-purchase interval, value, age, signup hour, purchase hour, signup weekend and purchase weekend. Device and IP counts include only strictly earlier purchase timestamps, excluding the current row and all simultaneous peers. One-hot vocabularies for sex, source and browser fit within each training fold; unseen categories are ignored.

The raw dataframe remains unchanged and features are derived from copies. Repeating feature construction cannot re-encode sex destructively. User ID, raw device/IP identifiers, purchase date, target, country and full-window frequencies are excluded. Earlier unlabeled events during validation and test can update counts for later transactions without model refitting.

The cost

Counts describe events visible in this sampled file and cannot recover omitted production history. Collection latency and subsequent field revisions lack verification logs. No static-window frequency comparator is added, keeping the three imbalance methods on identical event-time features.

The temporal roles and forward validation

The goal

Make temporal generalization the primary protocol while separating model/parameter choice, decision selection and frozen evaluation.

The temporal split

Purchases are sorted chronologically. Approximately the earliest 80% form the early pool, which is split again at about 80% into fit and selection. Entire timestamp groups move together at boundaries.

Five expanding folds within fit use unique timestamps; validation starts on March 16, 2015. Equal-weight mean fold AP selects the family and parameters. Fixed family and grid order resolves ties. Each family's best configuration is refitted on fit only, while selection chooses thresholds. Test evaluates all three predeclared frozen comparators and retains the CV-selected family.

The cost

A separate selection block reduces training data but separates decision choice from final evaluation. The time folds are not independent replicates, and their range is not a confidence interval. Prior full-data exploration prevents within-run isolation from restoring research-level blind-test independence.

The approval and rejection baselines

Approve-all and reject-all are defined before model selection. Scenario cost sums purchase value plus $15 for each missed fraud and adds $100 per rejected nonfraud. Correct decisions have zero modeled incremental error cost. These retained assumptions are not measured fraud losses or customer lifetime value.

On selection, approve-all costs $5,166 and reject-all $2,203,600, across 104 fraud and 22,036 nonfraud labels. No evidence supports imposing a minimum recall, review capacity or label-maturity delay. Precision is defined as zero when no transactions are flagged; TP/FP counts disclose the empty denominator.

The imbalance-treatment comparison

The goal

Test whether SMOTE improves temporal generalization under identical event-time features and evaluation roles.

The bounded comparison

The alternatives are no resampling, balanced training-fold class weights and ordinary SMOTE. Keeping LightGBM fixes the model family. Each method searches 200/400 trees, 31/63 leaves and learning rates of 0.05/0.1: eight configurations. Seed 42, four numerical threads and deterministic LightGBM settings are fixed. Encoding and sampling fit only on training folds. The comparison performs 120 fold fits and three fit-only refits.

The cost

The fit audit generates 86,200 synthetic records. Fractional category indicators occur in 82.67% and fractional weekend flags in 46.34%. Each sex indicator is fractional in about 40.02% of synthetic rows; counts also become nonintegral. In unscaled minority-neighbor squared distances, age contributes about 25.60% and value about 23.51%, showing scale-sensitive geometry. This implementation is retained for the audit; selective scaling would change the comparison. SMOTENC, ADASYN and additional model families are outside this experiment.

The selection decision boundaries

Threshold candidates cover every unique selection-score boundary plus explicit approve-all and reject-all decisions. With score >= threshold, an interval (lower, upper] between adjacent scores gives identical selection flags. Every threshold above the maximum approves all. An interior decision would freeze its upper endpoint. Cost ties favor fewer rejections, and all minimum-cost candidates remain available.

All three methods choose approve-all, each with one minimum-cost operational decision. These regions describe selection equivalence. The frozen policy is the constant approve-all action, not a finite threshold that might reject future scores above the selection maximum. Optimized selection cost is measured on the same data used to choose the decision and is not independent performance evidence.

Introducing rejection does not reduce selection cost under this scenario. Boundaries are compared using recall, precision, FPR, TP, FP and cost; the policy is selected before test evaluation.

This experiment compares direct approval and rejection; it does not simulate human review or its costs. The approve-all optimum means direct blocking has no cost advantage under these assumptions. Human review is a separate operating design, proposed in Section 12.

Random and temporal diagnostics

Only the temporal fit pool is used. Both diagnostics fix the selected weighted-model parameters and use 70,848 training rows with 17,712 held out. The random split is stratified and the chronological split holds out the later block. No further tuning occurs in the diagnostic. The inherited parameters were selected through forward CV over the entire fit pool, so these diagnostic holdouts are not independent of parameter selection.

Review prioritization and proposed three-tier handling

The following implementation proposals draw on the behavioral analysis and model comparison. Scores determine where reviewers look first, while transaction evidence informs individual decisions. Scores are not calibrated fraud probabilities.

Human-review queue

At submission, log the score, signup-to-purchase interval, prior device/IP activity and existing transaction details. Start by recording a shadow queue without changing order status to check data arrival, score distributions and logging completeness. For a trial, review orders in descending score order within a capacity agreed by the business; order waiting time can break score ties. Display rapid purchasing and unusual sharing as investigation cues, without making either an automatic rejection trigger.

Reviewers examine existing order and payment-verification records, account activity and device/IP links, then record a reason for their decision. Scores provide ordering, and verifiable transaction evidence supports case decisions. Shared devices or IPs can also occur in legitimate settings; further checks should address a specific concern.

Three-tier order handling

Low, medium and high denote proposed handling priorities, not calibrated probability bands. The business would set boundaries using selection-score distributions, actual review capacity and tolerance for false declines. Fixed probability cutoffs are not borrowed, and the current test is not used to tune them.

Proposed tier Candidate routing basis Suggested action Outcome records
Low risk Relatively low score and no additional verification cue in existing records Continue the existing standard process and retain sampled review Subsequent fraud labels, sample-review findings and order completion
Medium risk Score in the review range or a behavioral cue requiring a check Add to the standard human-review queue and examine the order and related activity Review decision, reason, handling time and customer wait
High risk Higher score accompanied by a verifiable anomaly Prioritize human review; shipment may be held under existing business authority, with human confirmation before release, further checks or rejection Final action, supporting evidence, cancellations/appeals and subsequent labels

Trial records and adjustment

Before a trial, agree and version the routing rules, review owner, capacity, waiting-time limits and decision authority. Track review hit rate, fraud coverage, legitimate-order interventions, handling time and order completion by date and behavioral subgroup. Sampled review of the standard flow helps reveal cases missed outside the queue.

Record label-confirmation and revision times alongside review outcomes. If queue yield, delays or false declines fall outside the business's acceptable range, reduce the queue or return to the existing process before adjusting from new records. The approve-all experiment remains a result under specified scenario costs, not an automatic-approval instruction. Review savings, detection gains and tier benefits remain to be measured.

Pilot data and operating metrics

The protocol uses raw-preserving transformations, strictly prior counts, chronological validation, matched imbalance comparisons and complete score-boundary decisions. Model choice, threshold selection, frozen test and exploratory diagnosis have separate roles.

Pilot preparation can organize three groups of information: label generation, confirmation, observation windows and revisions; first-order definitions, event arrival times and transaction history; and fraud losses, false-decline costs, review costs and capacity. The pilot can track user and device relationships, policy changes and review outcomes, then evaluate the model and workflow in new time windows.

No performance interval has been established for the dependence structure and selection process; metrics are disclosed as point estimates. Additional tuning cannot remove limitations that require new data.

First-order risk findings and implementation priorities

The analysis identifies a concentrated historical subgroup: 739 rapid purchases account for 52.23% of fraud labels and occur early in the data. Random-diagnostic AP is 0.635206, compared with 0.006314 after that subgroup is excluded. The aggregate random result therefore does not represent recognition of the remaining fraud cases. Behavioral subgroups and periods should be examined separately in risk analysis.

The matched comparison selects weighted LightGBM; ordinary SMOTE shows no AP gain in this experiment. Frozen temporal-test AP is 0.010121, above the window's 0.47% prevalence but still weak. Under the specified costs, the selected approve-all decision catches none of 131 fraud labels among 27,676 test transactions and incurs $6,876 scenario cost. Zero detection describes this operating decision; it neither means that every score is uninformative nor demonstrates effective human review.

The proposed application combines scores and behavioral cues to assist human review. Relative scores and verifiable concerns guide low/medium/high handling, with review outcomes, order effects and label revisions recorded over time. The deliverables are behavioral findings, model comparisons, decision trade-offs and a workflow suitable for a trial.

Implementation preparation should establish actual review capacity, decision authority, verification records and cost definitions. A trial can then collect new time windows and mature labels before changing tiers or models.

Appendix: Features and model settings

Data and Features

  • Inputs: imbalancedFraudDF.csv and IpAddress_to_Country.csv in the project directory.
  • Numeric features: interval_log, purchase_value, age, signup_hour, purchase_hour, signup_weekend, purchase_weekend, prior_device_count, prior_ip_count.
  • Categorical features: sex, source, browser; OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore", sparse_output=False), with vocabulary fitted on the current training fold only.
  • interval_log is log1p of seconds from signup to purchase. Prior device/IP counts include only transactions with strictly earlier purchase timestamps.

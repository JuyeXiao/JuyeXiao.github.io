Executive summary

Product count has a clear nonlinear association with churn: the rate is 7.6% for customers with two products and 82.7% for those with three. Inactive customers have a rate of 26.9%, compared with 14.3% for active customers. These differences prioritize investigation of product fit and barriers to service use. Age and geography differences provide further leads for needs interviews and regional service reviews.

Tree models outperform the linear and distance baselines on test ranking metrics. XGBoost+SMOTE achieves ROC-AUC 0.8706 and AP 0.7215, with random forest also a competitive reference. SHAP identifies age and product count as major contributors to this model's scores. Correct and incorrect examples show how explaining score formation helps identify model limitations.

Reducing the threshold from 0.5 to the selection-set cutoff of 0.275 expands the list from 459 to 827 customers, covering 93 additional churn labels while adding 275 false positives. The proposed implementation path matches the list to service capacity, investigates product fit and usage barriers, and then evaluates service proposals.

1 Framing the churn problem

This project addresses three retention decisions: which customer patterns warrant investigation, which methods better distinguish churn labels, and how to choose a candidate list within service capacity.

Grouped statistics identify patterns, model comparisons evaluate classification methods, and SHAP explains predictions and mistakes. The resulting proposals cover product-fit reviews, service guidance for inactive customers and segmented needs research.

2 Checking the data

The documented source is the public Kaggle dataset Churn Modeling, 10,000 rows by 14 columns, with no missing values. The target Exited records whether a customer has churned, at a rate of 20.4% (2,037 customers). Row number, customer id, and surname are dropped as identifiers before modeling.

The four-to-one class imbalance makes recall and PR-AUC useful complements to accuracy. This project compares SMOTE with class weighting; imbalance alone does not require resampling. Always predicting retention gives 79.6% accuracy while detecting no churners, so accuracy needs to be read alongside minority-class metrics.

The saved correlation heatmap shows generally weak pairwise linear correlations among numerical features, with age positively and activity negatively associated with Exited. Pairwise correlations do not rule out collinearity in the encoded design matrix or establish causality.

4 Preparing the features

The outer split is stratified 75/25 with seed 42. Of 7,500 outer-training rows, 1,500 form selection and 6,000 support fitting and CV. Selection/test rows do not fit preprocessing, SMOTE or hyperparameters. Geography one-hot encoding, Gender encoding and numeric scaling fit per training fold and transform its held-out fold. One-hot encoding avoids an ordering of countries; scaling supports KNN distances and LR regularization. Ordinary SMOTE interpolates in the encoded space, and the plausibility of synthetic category combinations has not been separately validated. Only 6,000 rows enter the final fit. Keeping selection rows out after threshold choice preserves the relationship between the fitted model’s scores and the cutoff, at the cost of fewer fitting rows.

The five searches use five-fold ROC-AUC for parameter selection, with 200 cross-validation fits and five best-configuration refits. The table evaluates fixed test scores at threshold 0.5; AP means average precision; the PR-curve legend uses PR-AUC for this same measure.

LR, KNN and RF provide linear, distance-based and tree-based reference models. Both XGBoost configurations have higher test ranking point estimates, but computational efficiency was not compared. Weighted/SMOTE point estimates are close, without a difference interval or equivalence test. Threshold analysis uses XGBoost+SMOTE as the prespecified reference model; test rankings do not change that choice.

Product-count and age patterns are nonlinear, and tree splits and interactions can represent such patterns. This is a plausible explanation for the tree models outperforming the current linear and distance baselines; no isolated ablation has quantified the gain from these patterns. Random forest is already competitive, so XGBoost should be considered alongside complexity and usage needs. Similar class-weighted results also show that SMOTE is not the only viable approach.

6 Setting the threshold and its cost

The maximum selection F₂ determines threshold 0.275, after which the model and threshold are frozen.

F₂ = 5 × P × R / (4 × P + R)

P is precision and R is recall, both entered as proportions from 0 to 1. F₂ is dimensionless and favors recall; its weighting does not specify an established real-world cost ratio.

Fixed threshold TP FP FN TN Flags Precision Recall
0.5 306 153 203 1,838 459 66.67% 60.12%
0.275 399 428 110 1,563 827 48.25% 78.39%

The two frozen policies are compared under this assumed loss:

L = FP + C × FN

C* = (428 − 153) / (203 − 110) = 2.956989

FP counts false positives and FN counts false negatives. One false positive costs 1 normalized unit. C is the assumed cost of one false negative relative to one false positive and is dimensionless; L is total normalized loss, not a currency amount. C* is the intersection of the two policy loss lines. Above 2.956989, threshold 0.275 has lower simulated loss than 0.5. This compares existing policies without selecting a new threshold on test.

The 2,000 fixed-prediction bootstrap resamples give 95% intervals of ROC-AUC [0.852,0.888], AP [0.685,0.757], recall [0.748,0.818] and precision [0.448,0.515]. They omit refitting, selection variation and future populations.

The direct operational difference is capacity: the lower threshold adds 368 potential contacts, covering 93 additional churn labels while adding 275 false positives. This quantifies the workload of a smaller, more precise list versus broader coverage, rather than identifying an optimum for every cost setting.

7 Model prediction basis and business interpretation

Model prediction basis: from inputs to contributions

TreeSHAP explains the fixed XGBoost+SMOTE scores for all test customers: positive contributions raise the churn score and negative contributions lower it. Numeric plots use source units. Global plots compare contribution magnitude, dependence plots show nonlinearity, and local plots contrast correct and incorrect predictions. Input mapping and score reconstruction are documented in the appendix.

The baseline log-odds is 0.010007, corresponding to a score of about 0.5025. It uses fitted-tree training path counts, including the SMOTE training distribution. It is neither population churn prevalence nor the average test prediction. Contributions depend on this model and baseline; its output scores have not been probability-calibrated.

Possible business explanations

Product combinations may reflect service needs, account lifecycle or product fit; activity may relate to service usage and engagement. Neither this dataset nor SHAP confirms those mechanisms. Age and geography can help audit score differences across customer groups, but do not independently justify incentives or outreach. These clues lead to the product-fit, usage-barrier and service-process proposals in the action table below.

8 Acting on the findings

The proposed priority is to investigate product fit and usage barriers among inactive customers, then use age-related needs and regional processes to locate specific issues. The table below proposes responsibilities and feedback measures to help decide whether to continue each approach.

Finding and target group Proposed action Suggested owner Feedback measures and next decision
High churn rates in the small groups with three or more products Review product combinations, opening sequence and service records; interview customers about duplication, fee clarity and fit with their needs, without prescribing fewer products Product manager and customer service Track feedback on duplication, fee understanding and unmet needs; recurring issues enter the product-information or service-process improvement list
Higher churn rate among inactive customers Use a short inquiry to distinguish usability difficulties, service barriers and reduced needs; provide guidance or problem resolution according to the response Customer operations and customer service Track useful response rate, barrier resolution, subsequent use and contact refusal; retain service approaches that solve specific problems
Marked age-group differences without a monotonic increase Combine age with product and activity information for segmented needs interviews; examine communication and service fit, using age for needs research User research and product manager Record needs, satisfaction and issue types by segment; adapt guidance and service rather than allocating incentives by age alone

Match a candidate list to service capacity

In this test sample, threshold 0.5 flags 459 customers with precision 66.67% and recall 60.12%, making it a candidate when staff capacity is tighter. Threshold 0.275 flags 827 customers with precision 48.25% and recall 78.39%, making it a candidate when more contacts are feasible and coverage matters more. Expanding the list adds 368 contacts, covers 93 additional churn labels and adds 275 false positives. This compares historical label coverage and workload; flagged customers are not retained customers.

Proposed sequence and deliverables

Start with a product-and-activity review list, consistent interview questions and a feedback classification sheet. Product and operations teams agree on service capacity and a candidate list policy. Local SHAP can help prepare questions about product use or service barriers; customer responses guide the conversation.

Next, trial service guidance or problem resolution and record contact, feedback, resolution and customer experience. If retention effects are evaluated, predefine the observation window and comparison method to measure incremental retention and net cost. Prepare a review list, a capacity-based list proposal and a service-trial evaluation sheet.

The data is a cross-sectional snapshot; group differences and model results should be interpreted alongside small product-group sizes and prediction errors.

9 Explanation methods

Explanation computation and input mapping

The explanation covers the fixed XGBoost+SMOTE model and all 2,500 test customers. The classifier receives 12 columns: three Geography indicators for France, Germany and Spain, an encoded Gender column, and eight standardized numeric columns: CreditScore, Age, Tenure, Balance, NumOfProducts, HasCrCard, IsActiveMember and EstimatedSalary. Gender uses Female=0 and Male=1; geography columns are 0/1 category indicators. Numeric plots show source values, with age in years and product count in units. Balance and salary retain source values without assuming a currency; tenure retains the recorded scale.

Column order comes from the fitted preprocessor, and inverse-transformed numbers are checked against each original row.

TreeSHAP uses tree_path_dependent explanations of raw log-odds. The baseline plus signed contributions reconstructs the model margin; sigmoid maps it to the model score. Across all test rows, maximum reconstruction errors are 2.86×10⁻⁶ for margins and 3.28×10⁻⁷ for scores. Positive contributions raise this model's churn score and negative contributions lower it.

Executive summary

This study asks which groups in public loan data face greater default risk and whether prediction models can help allocate limited review capacity. It selects 800,634 loans with elapsed terms and terminal outcomes, examines grouped default rates, effect sizes, binned trends and correlations, then excludes post-origination information and compares logistic regression, random forest and LightGBM using a temporal split. A separate selection set determines the model and threshold; policy costs are evaluated under alternative accounting assumptions. Equal-capacity rule comparisons, paired bootstrap intervals and SHAP explanations assess incremental ranking value and model behavior.

Default rates rise from 5.6% for grade A to 39.8% for grade G, while 60-month loans show 25.7% versus 14.4% for 36-month loans. LightGBM achieves the highest test AP, 0.315991. Its flagged group has a 25.17% default rate, approximately 1.60 times the overall test rate. However, the selected threshold flags 44.34% of loans: false positives leave gross simulated costs 7.63% lower but net costs 21.48% higher. At a fixed 10% capacity, LightGBM precision is 37.88%, versus 32.38% for the grade rule, with a 95% difference interval of 4.97–6.08 percentage points. SHAP identifies subgrade, recent account openings and repayment-burden features as major contributors. The resulting recommendation is to prioritize capacity-limited manual review, use grade, term and credit burden to guide checks, and select thresholds against missed defaults, false flags and review costs.

1 Loan risk and review decisions

This analysis uses 800,634 matured terminal-status loans to evaluate default ranking and threshold policies under fixed error prices. The 9,004-loan sample project uses a different population, with its own splits, prices and model evaluation.

The local parquet contains 2,260,668 loans and 151 columns, covering originations in 2007–2018. Fields describe loan terms, borrowers, credit records and repayment outcomes. The registered source is Kaggle: All Lending Club loan data.

2 Analysis sample and loan outcomes

Closed-loan comparisons by vintage require the share of loans without observed terminal outcomes.

The closed 2018 subset has 15.7% default versus 23.1% in 2017. At the April 2019 snapshot, recent loans mainly enter this subset through early repayment or charge-off; open_share rises from 32.5% in 2016 to 88.6% in 2018. Outcome-observation selection limits the comparison: it neither establishes improving risk nor quantitatively excludes real risk changes.

The code requires elapsed months to cover the full term: 36-month loans through April 2016 and 60-month loans through April 2014, followed by Fully Paid/Charged Off filtering. The 800,634-loan cohort has 15.4% default, with vintage rates of 12.6%–17.9%. This limits recent-vintage censoring without guaranteeing unbiased selection. Later loans are excluded from model evaluation.

Exclusions comprise 1,440,841 loans with unelapsed terms, 2,749 credit-policy labels and 16,444 matured non-final loans. The last group represents 12.1% of matured 2016 loans and at most 0.7% elsewhere. This affects outcome availability and does not establish negligible aggregate selection effects.

The cohort defaults at 15.4%, a class imbalance near 5.5:1. By term, 36-month loans default at 14.4% (727,819 loans) and 60-month loans at 25.7% (72,815 loans), nearly twice as high. Joint applications are 0.2% of the cohort (1,884 loans); they keep their individual-level income and DTI fields and are retained as ordinary rows.

Prices are estimated from fit outcomes; Section 6 explains gross and net accounting and the cost calculation.

3 Loan risk patterns and group differences

The risk signals below describe the full matured terminal cohort. The 9,004-loan sample analysis provides distribution and model comparisons for a separate population, using its own evaluation protocol.

EDA describes observed outcomes in the matured terminal-status cohort, subject to its exclusions. FICO and grouped associations characterize cohort risk but do not directly measure predictive increments.

Default rises from grade A at 5.6% to G at 39.8%; small_business is 24.6%, versus about 12%–13% for weddings, cars and credit-card purposes. Employment-length groups span 15.2%–15.7% for one to nine years and 14.3% for ten-plus. A narrow group range does not establish absence of signal or equivalence.

Across about 800,000 rows, Welch p-values display as 0.000 after three-decimal rounding; they are neither exact zero nor devoid of information. Effect sizes include interest rate 0.598, FICO −0.363, accounts opened in 24 months 0.273 and mortgage accounts −0.181. FICO is absent from the current sample project’s 30 columns. These mean differences do not establish predictive increments, causal effects or pricing mechanisms.

Bin-order versus bin-default-rate Spearman correlations have magnitude 1.00 for interest rate, FICO, utilization and DTI, with FICO decreasing. qcut targets deciles and merges duplicate boundaries. These group trends do not establish individual monotonicity or exclude protective effects within particular ranges. Keeping continuous inputs does not prove binning unhelpful.

Three pairs exceed |r|>0.5: amount/installment 0.98, open/total accounts 0.69 and public/bankruptcy records 0.63. Installment is calculated from amount, rate and term, and section 4 drops some related columns. Lower remaining pairwise correlations do not guarantee stable logistic coefficients or absence of multivariate collinearity.

Grades E–G account for 6.7% of loans and 14.1% of default counts, a share ratio near 2.1, versus 9.4%/22.0% in the parent. These are not monetary-loss or recoverable-loss shares. State default rates of 10.0% (DC) to 19.2% (MS) describe this cohort without isolating geographic mechanisms.

4 Predictor selection and feature processing

A default predictor must use information available when a decision is made. This step checks the original 151 columns, excludes subsequent repayment and outcome information, and then handles missing values, categories and engineered features. Using later information is called data leakage: it can inflate offline performance while being unavailable for new-loan predictions. The issue here is the timing of predictive information, not a disclosure of personal data.

Missingness and near-constant screening fit within each training fold. Median imputation, encoding, LR scaling, TargetEncoder and class weights follow the same role boundaries, preventing selection/test distributions or labels from entering fitting. The final fit selects 57 candidate fields; each fold determines its own count.

Field exclusions limit post-origination information; quality screening does not independently establish approval-time availability. Credit-history, loan-to-income and installment-to-income features use fixed definitions. Class weights address imbalance; this cohort has no SMOTE ablation establishing which approach is better.

5 Temporal splitting and model comparison

A July-2015 boundary holds out later loans, while earlier training is split into fit and selection. Parameters are selected by three-fold average precision on a stratified sample of at most 200,000 fit rows. Logistic regression, random forest and LightGBM compare linear and tree-based approaches. Selection chooses model and threshold under the recall constraint, without merging selection into a refit. The separate selection role reduces final training size; stratified inner folds are not forward-chaining validation. The previously inspected test supports conditional retrospective evaluation.

AP means average precision. Test point estimates establish neither significant model differences nor deployment readiness.

LightGBM improves test AP by approximately 0.0164 over Logistic and 0.0112 over Random Forest. Recall is similar across the three models, so the visible advantage is ranking quality rather than substantially greater coverage at the selected thresholds. Trees can represent nonlinearities and interactions, a plausible methodological explanation, Section 7 uses SHAP to explain model outputs, while the source of ranking gains remains untested by ablation. LightGBM is a candidate ranking model, with Logistic retained to compare ranking benefits against maintenance costs.

Rules and models at equal review capacity

The primary comparison fixes review volume at 10% of each batch, with 5% and 20% capacity references. Candidate rules rank by worse grade, lower FICO or higher DTI; selection precision at 10% chooses the grade rule. A fixed, label-independent hash ordering breaks ties. Logistic and LightGBM use frozen model scores. Every method flags the same number of loans in the same test set, comparing detection at equal workload.

Method Flagged loans Defaults found Precision Recall Net simulated cost reduction
Grade rule 27,147 8,790 32.38% 20.57% 1.74%

LightGBM 27,147 10,282 37.88% 24.06% 6.76%

Within the same 27,147-loan queue, LightGBM identifies 1,492 more defaults than the grade rule and 469 more than Logistic. It has higher precision and net simulated cost reduction at all three evaluated capacities, supporting it as a review-ranking candidate. At 10% capacity, roughly 76% of defaults remain outside the queue: this is a resource-constrained prioritization policy, not a high-recall policy.

Fixed-volume ranking produces a threshold that varies with each batch's score distribution. It differs from the main fixed 0.46 threshold and its high-recall objective. The 10% capacity was specified before evaluation, not optimized on test outcomes. Simulated costs use fit-derived average prices to compare policies at fixed capacity.

Paired bootstrap: conditional uncertainty in differences

Labels and the three methods' fixed flags for the same loans are jointly resampled 2,000 times. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of each difference form a 95% interval. Multinomial sampling over joint label-and-flag categories is exactly equivalent to paired row resampling for these count-based metrics and preserves correspondence across methods.

LightGBM versus Precision difference (pp) 95% interval Net cost reduction difference (pp) 95% interval
Grade rule 5.50 4.97–6.08 5.02 4.55–5.56
Logistic 1.73 1.34–2.14 1.58 1.23–1.95

At the primary capacity, these intervals lie above zero, supporting LightGBM's advantage in this fixed-prediction comparison. Intervals assume IID loans and condition on models, prices, rule selection and original queue membership. Each replicate neither reranks loans nor retrains models; tuning uncertainty and temporal dependence are excluded, and multiple intervals are not adjusted for simultaneous inference.

6 Cost assumptions and threshold selection

Fit outcomes estimate the cost of each missed default at approximately $8,550 gross or $5,856 net. Each falsely flagged repaid loan carries an estimated cost of approximately $2,567.

Total simulated cost = Missed defaults × Cost per missed default + False flags × Cost per false flag

Substituting the gross or net missed-default prices estimated from fit data yields the corresponding simulated costs, showing how price assumptions affect policy trade-offs.

With the LightGBM threshold set to 0.46, the policy flags 44.34% of test loans. It reduces simulated costs under gross accounting but increases them under net accounting.

7 Risk ranking, false-positive costs and model explanations

The model concentrates higher-risk loans. The test set contains 271,471 loans, of which 42,739 defaulted. The overall default rate is 15.74%.

The model flags 120,374 loans, including 30,304 defaults. The flagged group has a 25.17% default rate, approximately 1.60 times the overall rate. This supports review prioritization, although roughly three quarters of flagged loans ultimately repaid normally.

False flags explain the policy cost. Each detected default comes with approximately 2.97 normally repaid loans falsely flagged. Under the fixed-price model, improvement relative to approving all loans is:

Cost improvement = Correctly flagged defaults × Avoided cost per default − False flags × Cost per false flag

Under gross prices, each detected default can offset approximately 3.33 false flags; under net prices, only 2.28. The observed ratio of 2.97 lies between them, explaining why the same policy helps under gross accounting and hurts under net accounting. This explains the simulation; it does not assume manual review can actually prevent every associated loss.

Thresholds must reflect the objective. A threshold of 0.46 offers greater coverage and workload. The frozen net-cost scenario without a recall floor uses 0.70, flags 5.64%, and recalls only 14.87%. These scenarios show trade-offs, not a future optimal threshold selected from test performance.

SHAP: how the model forms its predictions

The frozen LightGBM is explained using 128 fit loans as background and 1,024 test loans chosen by fixed hash ordering. TreeExplainer uses interventional feature perturbation and raw log-odds output. Home-ownership one-hot contributions are summed per loan before mean absolute aggregation. The maximum difference between reconstructed and model raw outputs is 1.25×10⁻⁷, passing the additivity check. Scores are not probability-calibrated.

The largest mean absolute contributions come from subgrade, accounts opened in the past 24 months, DTI, loan-to-income and home ownership. The model therefore uses recent credit expansion and repayment-burden information alongside existing credit ratings. SHAP explains score construction; separate predictive gains from each input would still require ablation.

Four cases use the existing 0.46 threshold to define TP, FP, FN and TN. Within the fixed explanation sample, each case is the loan closest to its group's median score, avoiding a selection of only narratively convenient extremes. Red contributions raise raw output and blue contributions lower it. Units are log-odds, not default-probability percentage points.

  • Correctly flagged default (TP, score 0.617): DTI, subgrade and FICO raise the score, partially offset by other inputs.
  • Falsely flagged repaid loan (FP, score 0.565): subgrade, months since the most recent bankcard account and FICO raise the score, but the loan ultimately repaid normally. Risk attributes do not determine individual outcomes.
  • Missed default (FN, score 0.386): recent account openings and months since the most recent bankcard account contribute negatively; positive contributions do not lift the score above the threshold.
  • Correctly unflagged repaid loan (TN, score 0.292): recent openings, loan-to-income, subgrade and DTI contribute to a lower score.

Correlated inputs such as grade and subgrade may share attribution; target encoding also affects interpretation. Every temporal test loan has a 36-month term, but the background contains other terms, so term can still contribute relative to that background. This does not mean term differentiates loans within the test window. Attribution explains the model rather than causal effects or automatic rejection reasons.

8 Model performance across temporal splits

The random reference has its own fit/selection/test and selection process, without inheriting temporal hyperparameters. Its test ROC-AUC=0.709826, AP=0.303814 and threshold=0.45. Its 68,271 post-July-2015 test rows give ROC-AUC=0.719401 and AP=0.322362. Temporal ROC-AUC=0.717803 covers 271,471 rows, so these are not paired comparisons on identical loans.

Training sizes, composition, periods and test rows change together. Differences cannot price drift or establish its absence. A single later test window cannot certify generalization to other years, regions or rejected applicants.

These recommendations inform the design of a risk-review pilot. Start with risk segmentation and queue display, record review outcomes, and assess default coverage and costs as loan outcomes mature. Monitor origination vintages so that incomplete outcomes are not mistaken for declining risk.

The analysis covers historical approved loans with matured terminal outcomes; the temporal test window contains only 36-month loans. Group associations, fixed-price simulations and a single later window limit transferability. The proposals can guide a pilot; business effects remain to be observed.

Executive summary

The course analysis uses 18,219 Oakland observations to identify pollutant patterns by road distance and land use. Median NO₂ is 23.8 within 0–50 m of a motorway and 7.5 beyond 1 km, both reported on the course file’s original numerical scale. This relative difference supports road-distance features and distance-stratified sampling.

Within the tested candidates and selection procedure, random forest outperforms histogram gradient boosting on tract hold-outs, with pooled NO₂ R² of 0.762083 and 0.730200. Random-forest scores are 0.942353, 0.762083 and 0.576017 for random-point, census-tract and coordinate-region hold-outs. Predicting new regions is therefore harder than filling gaps within the sampled area. Industrial-zone NO₂ MAE is 4.283, compared with 2.219 in residential zones, making industrial errors a priority for diagnosis.

The relative prediction map covers 628.74 km, or 36.79% of the centerline network, across 25,746 segments. Across 70 tracts, predicted NO₂ and the respiratory-hazard category have a rank correlation of 0.707. These findings inform a proposed plan to screen investigation candidates using grades and model agreement, inspect industrial errors, and sample uncovered areas.

Spatial prediction tasks

The project asks three questions: how much predictive information comes from road distances and surrounding context, how well models perform at held-out locations, and how their predictions can be presented as comparable road maps.

NO₂, NO and PM₂.₅ are modeled separately using the course dataset. Random-point hold-outs evaluate new points within the sampled area; census-tract hold-outs evaluate unseen tracts; coordinate-region hold-outs test transfer across larger contiguous areas. Evaluation, mapping and error analysis demonstrate a spatial data-analysis workflow.

Observation data and road network

The supplied course dataset contains 18,286 rows and 17 columns describing pollutants, coordinates, road classes, land-use zones and environmental context. Removing rows with missing zone, wind or temperature leaves 18,222 rows; deduplication retains 18,219 observations across 70 census tracts.

NO (nitric oxide) has a long right tail. Large values are retained rather than removed solely for their magnitude, so their influence remains visible in distribution plots and prediction errors.

The local OpenStreetMap network contains 22,137 directed edges. A projected centerline union avoids counting reversed and overlapping roads twice, producing 1,709.21 km of roads. The network within 50 m of observation points totals 591.19 km (34.59%), describing the spatial coverage of the observations.

Road data come from OpenStreetMap, under ODbL, © OpenStreetMap contributors. Pollutants are analyzed on the original numerical scale of the course file.

Road distances and context features

Six features measure distance to the nearest motorway, primary road, secondary road, tertiary road, traffic signal and stop sign. A metric projection and sjoin_nearest support the queries. Observations and prediction midpoints share build_features() so the definitions agree. Across 100 sampled points, all six distances match individual geometry calculations, with maximum absolute disagreement of 0.00e+00 m.

Road class and zone use one-hot encoding. Distances, wind, temperature and population density remain continuous. Training and prediction matrices share the same column names and order.

The nearest OpenStreetMap road class agrees with the file's road_type at 95.3% of observations. For 370 points, the file records secondary roads while the nearest network road is primary. Prediction segments use their centerline class; conflicting classes remain ungraded. This comparison illustrates attribute checks after spatial joins.

Nested spatial evaluation

Random-point, GEOID-tract and coordinate-region protocols each use five outer folds with five inner folds. Fifteen outer and 75 inner splits trace the same 18,219 rows, excluding outer test rows from parameter choices, ablation and fold refits. Tract and region protocols keep each spatial group entirely in training or testing.

Selection searches six RF and four HGB parameter settings using mean inner-fold NO₂ R² on all 18 features, then compares four feature sets using RF pooled inner R². NO₂ choices transfer to other targets; HGB uses the RF-selected feature set. This limits experiment size but does not independently optimize features for HGB or the other targets. Parameter and sequential feature choices are nested, totaling 1,096 real fits including final map selection/fitting. The protocols define different spatial prediction tasks, so their results are reported separately.

Road distance and pollutant distributions

Grouping observations by distance to the nearest motorway reveals how pollutant distributions vary with distance. Median NO₂ is 23.8 at 0–50 m, 15.1 at 50–100 m, 13.3 at 250–300 m and 7.5 beyond 1 km. Higher values in nearby groups support including road distance as a candidate predictive feature.

The binned comparison preserves the observation distribution, showing both trend and spread. Road classes and surrounding conditions also differ across distance groups, so this comparison describes sample associations and informs stratified sampling.

Prediction coverage and relative grades

The surface construction first intersects projected centerline unions with the 300 m polygon-buffer union, then segments and takes midpoints. Buffers use 32 segments per quadrant and overlay tolerance 1e-6 m. HTML and static maps use the same 25,746 clipped geometries, each at most 50 m long. The 300 m limit controls spatial extrapolation.

The same six final models predict at each midpoint. Each pollutant's prediction-point quintiles define grades, then the worst grade is retained. Tract associations with the health field

Respiratory_HI is treated as the course dataset's respiratory-hazard category for an exploratory comparison with pollutant predictions. It is excluded from the pollutant models. Each census tract contributes one row: its category is the mode across observations, and low, moderate and high are ranked 1, 2 and 3 for a Spearman comparison.

The surface follows the course aggregation: take the arithmetic mean of NO₂ prediction points grouped by the tract identifier borrowed from their nearest observation. Across 70 tracts, the rank correlation is 0.707. The low, moderate and high groups contain 10, 16 and 44 tracts, with median tract mean predictions of 7.547, 10.908 and 14.644.

Using tract means of observed NO₂ gives a rank correlation of 0.839; using tract means of held-out predictions at those observation locations gives 0.823. The consistent ordering demonstrates aggregation, ordinal coding and rank association within the dataset. The surface correlation is lower, and its comparison locations also change from observations to road midpoints.

Tract associations use fields within the dataset and tract IDs assigned from the nearest observation. A follow-up comparison can clip roads to actual tract boundaries and evaluate the two spatial aggregation methods.

Zone errors and spatial diagnostics

Error diagnostics use RF NO₂ predictions from tract hold-outs. Residuals are observed minus predicted values: positive means underprediction and negative means overprediction. The overall mean residual is −0.381155, its standard deviation is 4.396357, and MAE is 2.969.

Industrial zones have nearly zero mean residual but the largest MAE, showing how overprediction and underprediction can cancel. Residential zones have lower MAE. Open-space predictions are high on average, though that group contains only 224 observations.

The spatial residual display uses a fixed random sample of 5,000 observations. The group table and metrics use all 18,219 points. A useful next step is to inspect high-error industrial roads, comparing road classes, distances and observed-value distributions before adding features or changing the model.

Project findings and next steps

Road distance helps identify spatial pollutant patterns, while model suitability depends on where predictions are needed. Higher median NO₂ near motorways indicates that distance carries prediction-relevant information. Under the tested selection procedure, RF performs better than HGB on tract hold-outs, supporting RF as the main mapping model and HGB as a check on grade stability. The comparison supports this choice without isolating an algorithmic mechanism behind the advantage.

RF pooled NO₂ R² falls from 0.942353 for random-point hold-outs to 0.576017 for coordinate-region hold-outs, showing that transfer across regions is harder. Spatial proximity and changing feature distributions are explanations to investigate; their separate contributions have not been quantified. Industrial MAE of 4.283 versus residential MAE of 2.219 identifies where improvement is needed. The near-zero industrial mean residual reflects cancellation between positive and negative errors and cannot replace absolute-error diagnostics.

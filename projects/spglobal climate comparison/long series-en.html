1. Long-series analysis summary

This report uses observation and model records spanning 2000–2024 to compare short-term forecasting methods, examine observed trends and assess bias-correction stability. Forecasts use five test windows and two horizons, with imputation and parameter selection confined to training. Calibration is assessed on a common recent holdout and under alternative windows and model ensembles.

Short-term forecasting requires a choice by region and horizon. Monthly climatology remains the default comparison for CA and XJ; Prophet in TK and 24-month SARIMA in AU merit further validation. Long-term assessment uses differences paired within each climate model. Scenario temperature differences are easier to interpret directly than absolute exceedance counts, which respond to calibration windows and historical frequency bias.

2. Uses of the longer record

A longer record permits comparisons across forecast windows, tests of trend endpoint sensitivity and checks of correction stability. Record length alone does not establish model suitability, and residual diagnostics cannot define a universal twenty-year minimum. Cell-level validation and exposure screening are presented in the scenario and spatial exposure report.

3. Extending both sides of the record

3.1 Observations: 299 months per region

MODIS daytime land surface temperature now covers 2000-02 to 2024-12 in all four regions, 299 months each. The coverage rule (at least 50% of grid points per day, at least 10 valid days per month) costs a different amount in each:

Region Valid months Masked Masked share
CA 286 13 4.3%
XJ 278 21 7.0%
TK 245 54 18.1%
AU 292 7 2.3%

TK loses close to one month in five, and the losses concentrate in the cold season: 50 of its 54 masked months fall between December and March. That warm-season lean follows every TK figure in this report.

3.2 Consistency in the overlapping period

The six-year and long-record extractions both cover 2015–2020. Pairing originally observed months gives 69, 67, 60 and 72 months in CA, XJ, TK and AU. The maximum absolute difference is 0.0000 °C, correlation is 1.000000, and masked-month counts agree in every region. This supports consistent data definitions in the overlapping period.

3.3 Model series: historical joined to SSP

NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 distributes each model's SSP run from 2015 onward. Before that comes the historical run, which in CMIP6 is the simulation the SSP run continues from. Joining the two at 2014–2015 makes the model series as long as the observations.

Two checks establish that the join is legitimate. All fifteen models carry the same ensemble-member label and the same file version on both sides, and every spliced series is 300 months long in every region. The seam itself is measured as the difference between the five-year climatologies of the same calendar month on either side. The other fourteen models sit at +0.28 ± 0.18 °C, a range of −0.15 to +0.50; CMCC-CM2-SR5 sits at −0.13 °C, 2.2 standard deviations away.

4. Method: the splice, the gaps, and the test design

Four decisions, each with its purpose, the alternatives, and what it costs.

4.1 The splice

The goal

Give the model series a window as long as the observations.

The choice

The alternative is to use the SSP segment alone and confine the analysis to 2015 onward, which leaves a window of six to ten years. Joining the historical segment is legitimate only if both segments are the same simulation, so the ensemble-member label and the file version are checked model by model, and all fifteen agree.

The cost

The scenario branch begins only in 2015, so 2000–2014 is one line shared by all scenarios. Every long-window result in this report is therefore scenario-agnostic, and any comparison between scenarios still has to be made after 2015.

4.2 Missingness by use

Historical trend diagnostics compare original NaNs, interpolation and complete calendar months. Forecasting splits the test window before training-only filling and excludes originally missing test months from scoring. Correction and frequency comparisons retain original masks and the full calendar; dropping missing rows must not turn nonadjacent months into neighbors.

4.3 Forecast windows and aggregation

Monthly climatology predicts the training-period mean for each calendar month. Seasonal naive repeats the final 12 months of the training series. Lower root mean square error (RMSE) indicates closer agreement with observations.

The five test end years are 2016, 2018, 2020, 2022 and 2024, each with a 12- and 24-month window looking backwards. Within each horizon all four methods are scored on identical originally observed months. The two horizons have different forecast origins. Splitting precedes training-only imputation and parameter selection, preventing future observations from influencing training.

Full results and the two RMSE aggregations appear in §5.

4.4 Calibration and test split for the correction

The goal

Measure how the correction behaves outside the window it was fitted on, over a span long enough to distinguish it from a climatology.

The procedure

Twelve monthly offsets are fitted on 2000–2013 and tested from 2014 onward, scoring 113 to 129 months per region. The same offsets are then fitted separately on two decades and compared directly in level and in shape.

The cost

The calibration period falls entirely inside the historical segment while the test period is mostly the SSP segment. This split tests whether offsets fitted in the historical segment transfer to the scenario segment.

Prophet in TK and 24-month SARIMA in AU therefore remain candidates to test at new forecast origins.

Mean-window RMSE averages the RMSE calculated separately in each test window. Pooled RMSE combines squared errors across all scored months, takes their mean, then takes the square root. For AU's 12-month forecasts, this choice changes the relative order of Prophet and SARIMA.

Method Mean-window RMSE (°C) Pooled RMSE (°C)
Prophet 2.895 3.086
SARIMA 2.982 3.035

Prophet has lower mean-window error, while SARIMA has lower pooled error. The aggregation method therefore needs to be specified before comparing models.

MASE uses originally observed 12-month seasonal pairs in training for its denominator. MASE below 1 does not replace a direct test-period comparison with seasonal naive. Five windows establish neither statistical equivalence nor a universal twenty-year minimum for SARIMA.

Next, add new test end years at each specified horizon, fixing the scoring mask and aggregation in advance. Track error differences, window wins and residuals before replacing the default baseline.

6. Trends: what survives four checks

The following trend diagnostics describe the observed LST record under the specified windows and missing-data treatments.

Trend interpretation examines gap treatment, time endpoints, slope intervals and day–night records.

6.1 Gap treatment: two regions change verdict

CA returns no trend under all three treatments. XJ is significant only when the gaps are interpolated (p = 0.0150), against 0.0796 with gaps kept as NaN and 0.0656 on complete calendar months. TK is significant with gaps kept as NaN (0.0217) and interpolated (0.0016) but not on complete months (0.0630). AU is significant under all three.

6.2 Window endpoints: AU's significance comes from the last five years

Sen slopes by sub-window, with an asterisk for p < 0.05: AU gives −0.89* over 2000–2024, +0.20 over 2000–2019, −1.22* over 2005–2024 and −1.25* over 2010–2024. Removing the last five years changes the sign and removes the significance. TK has the same shape, at +0.19 over 2000–2014 against −1.47* over 2010–2024.

6.3 The slope intervals agree with the p-values

Theil-Sen 95% intervals are −0.058 [−0.360, +0.254] in CA, −0.302 [−0.615, +0.034] in XJ, −0.460 [−0.833, −0.077] in TK and −0.891 [−1.223, −0.556] in AU. TK and AU exclude zero, CA and XJ include it, each consistent with its own p-value.

6.4 Day–night records and mechanism testing

Night-time land surface temperature trends are +0.608 °C/decade in CA, +0.699 in XJ, +0.416 in TK and +0.266 in AU, all at p ≤ 0.002, opposite in sign to the day side.

Day–night differences motivate checks of observation conditions and sampling. Terra timing changes are a candidate mechanism, not an established attribution. Night data use looser coverage rules. Aqua, matched locations and seasons, and observation-time matching can help distinguish explanations.

7. Bias correction: long window, ensemble, lead time

Sections 7.1–7.3 evaluate regional means using 15 models. The common-holdout results for cell correction use the main 14-model ensemble and appear in synthesis Section 3. Lower ensemble-mean temperature RMSE does not justify averaging temperature before a nonlinear threshold.

7.3 Historical offset differences

Fitting the same twelve monthly offsets separately on two decades and comparing them gives median level shifts of −0.84 °C in AU, −0.54 in TK, −0.38 in XJ and −0.19 in CA, with median shape correlations of 0.99, 0.87, 0.98 and 0.98. Against a seasonal range of 10.8 to 16.7 °C in the offset itself, the first three regions drift by a few percent of it.

The largest single-month difference exposes the exception: 1.62 °C in XJ, 2.50 in AU, 2.90 in CA and 6.94 in TK. TK's cold-season offsets rest on very few years, which follows directly from the 50 cold-season masked months of §3.1.

7.4 Common holdout and calibration endpoints

Calibration windows change some directions. AU near-window population “future minus observed” moves from −0.125 under the baseline to +0.028 through +0.210 months/year across six calibration settings. TK population-to-area frequency ratios move from 1.005 to 0.931–0.979. XJ moves from 0.904 to 1.025 with the offset ending in 2013. CA stays above 1 under these calibration windows but reaches 0.844 under an assumed −3 °C shift. Ratios, rankings and lists must therefore be interpreted with their parameter settings.

Moving the calibration endpoint later does not reduce error monotonically. A single endpoint experiment cannot establish future error bounds. NASA Terra orbit information supports examining observation-time changes alongside clear-sky sampling, regional change and interannual variability. Aqua, matched locations and seasons, and overpass-time matching can help distinguish these candidate mechanisms.

Record calibration windows, population sources and member influence separately. Whole-year resampling does not cover multiyear dependence or future nonstationarity; ±1 °C and ±3 °C shifts are stress settings. These ranges identify sensitive candidates rather than provide complete future predictive coverage.

8. Model ensemble and data versions

CMCC-CM2-SR5 is available only as unrevised v1.0 data and has a temperature-offset anomaly in its historical and ssp585 periods. The main ensemble uses the other 14 models, with all 15 included in a separate sensitivity analysis. Its apparent RMSE advantage is not a model-selection criterion.

9. Further tests of forecasts and calibration

Add test years to compare candidate forecasting methods with climatology on the same scored months. Calibration tests should examine mean error, exceedance frequency and window sensitivity together. Independent observations are still needed to distinguish Terra observation timing, clear-sky sampling and regional change; the trend diagnostics do not establish attribution.
